From Cherry to Cup

Every cup of coffee starts as a red fruit on a forest shrub. How that shrub is grown decides what your cup costs the forest, and how it tastes.

Coffee is a forest plant. Wild arabica still grows in the shade of the mountain forests of Ethiopia [1], and in India most coffee is grown under a canopy of trees [2]. Here is how a coffee berry becomes your morning cup, and why it matters whether it grew under living forest or in open sun.

The journey of a coffee bean

Each step below happens on a coffee plantation in the Western Ghats, through one year.

  1. 1 · February to April

    Blossom showers

    The first good rains after the dry months bring the bushes into flower all at once. For two or three days the plantation smells of jasmine, and bees work the blossom.

  2. 2 · April to October

    Green berries

    Each pollinated flower becomes a berry that grows slowly through the monsoon. Under shade it ripens more slowly, which gives the bean time to develop its flavour [5].

  3. 3 · November to February

    Ripe cherries

    The berries turn red and sweet. Coffee growers call them cherries. Inside each are usually two seeds, the coffee beans.

  4. 4 · Harvest

    Picking by hand

    Pickers go over the bushes several times, taking the ripe cherries. Fallen and leftover berries are gathered too, so that pests have nowhere to wait for next year.

  5. 5 · After picking

    Drying in the sun

    We dry our ripe cherries whole in the sun, turning and raking them for two to three weeks. This gives what is called cherry coffee, or natural-process coffee. Some estates instead pulp and wash the cherries first, leaving the bean in a papery skin (parchment coffee).

  6. 6 · Curing and roasting

    Green beans to roast

    At the curing works the dry husk is removed and the green beans are sorted by size. Roasting, usually far from the plantation, turns them brown and brings out the aroma you know.

Shade or sun: the same drink, two different landscapes

Coffee can be grown under a canopy of trees, or in open sun with the trees cut down. Sun coffee can give more beans for a few years, but it needs more fertiliser and pesticide. Across Latin America, studies of ants, birds and trees found that species are lost step by step as shade is thinned and removed, and that coffee grown under native forest canopy can hold as many ant and bird species as nearby forest [3]. In Kodagu, next door to us, native shade trees on many estates have been replaced by a single fast-growing exotic tree, silver oak (Grevillea robusta). Where silver oak rose from about a third to over half of the trees, the Malabar barbet, a bird found only in this region, became far scarcer [4].

Coffee under native shade

  • Many kinds of trees, birds, frogs and insects
  • Leaf litter feeds the soil and holds water
  • Cooler, steadier conditions for the bushes
  • Natural pest control and pollination

Sun coffee

  • Few trees, few species
  • More fertiliser and pesticide
  • Bare soil that dries out and washes away
  • Bushes exposed to heat and drought

What the forest does for your coffee

Biodiversity is not just pleasant to have around. The animals and trees of a shaded plantation do work that the coffee depends on.

Bees pollinate it

In Costa Rica, wild bees from nearby forest raised coffee yields by up to 20% on bushes within a kilometre of the forest, and reduced misshapen beans [6].

Birds and ants guard it

Insect-eating birds cut damage from the coffee berry borer on farms in Jamaica and Costa Rica, and farms with more forest nearby had more birds [7, 8]. See how this works on our Pest Outbreak page.

Trees shelter it

Shade trees even out the swings of heat and humidity around the bushes, which helps coffee cope with hotter, less predictable weather [9].

Slower ripening, better cup

Shade slows the ripening of the berries, and in warmer sites this has been shown to improve bean quality and taste [5].

Why organic matters too

Shade gives wildlife a home; organic farming keeps that home safe. Across many studies, organic farms held about 30% more species than conventional farms [10]. Broad-spectrum insecticides kill the wasps, spiders, ants and lady beetles that keep pests in check, and harm frogs as well [11]. Pests then often come back in greater numbers than before. How we grow coffee at Endemic Greens

Our coffee grows organically under native shade trees of the Western Ghats, alongside pepper and cardamom. We feed the soil with Jeevamrutha and leaf litter instead of chemical fertiliser, and our nursery raises saplings of native trees to bring back species that have become rare. Our tree survey and wildlife stories show what lives under the canopy. We sell our coffee, pepper and cardamom to wholesale buyers only.

What you can do as a coffee drinker

  • Ask where your coffee comes from. A good roaster or café can tell you the country, the region and often the estate.
  • Look for shade-grown and organic coffee. Labels such as organic and Bird Friendly certify how coffee is grown; shade grown under native trees is best for wildlife.
  • Try Indian coffee. Most of it is grown under a two-tier canopy of trees, often alongside pepper and cardamom [2].
  • Share what you learn. Every cup is a vote for the kind of landscape that grows it.
